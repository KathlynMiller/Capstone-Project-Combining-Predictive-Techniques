**Concept Reflection — Combining Predictive Techniques**
**Kathlyn Miller-Francis | BANA 622 | April 27, 2026**

---

This project required applying several predictive analytics concepts in sequence — data cleaning, feature engineering, model training, and evaluation — to a realistic, messy business dataset. Reflecting on the process reveals how each concept connects to the others and why no single step can be treated in isolation.

**Data leakage and the importance of the split boundary.** The most consequential design decision was identifying and removing leakage columns before any modeling. Variables like interest rate and loan balance are only assigned *after* Lending Club grades the loan, so including them would give the model the answer it is supposed to predict. This concept — that training data must reflect only what is known at prediction time — is easy to state but surprisingly hard to apply. The 80/20 train-test split was performed *before* fitting any scaler or imputer for the same reason: fitting preprocessing on the full dataset would allow test-set statistics to leak into training, inflating apparent performance.

**Missing data as signal, not noise.** Two columns — `months_since_last_delinq` and `months_since_90d_late` — were missing for the majority of borrowers not because of data quality problems but because those borrowers had *never* experienced those events. Treating the absence as information (adding binary `ever_delinq` and `ever_90d_late` flags) and filling with a sentinel value (999) preserved that signal rather than collapsing it into a median that would imply the event happened at some typical time. This distinction — structural missingness versus random missingness — is a concept that applies in nearly every real-world dataset.

**Model selection under class imbalance.** With only 18.5% positive cases, a naive classifier that always predicts "prime" achieves 81.5% accuracy while being completely useless. This is why ROC-AUC and F1 score were prioritized over accuracy. The Random Forest's high precision (0.667) means its positive predictions are reliable, but its low recall (0.032) at the default threshold illustrates a core trade-off: where to draw the decision boundary depends on the relative cost of false positives and false negatives, a business judgment that no metric can make automatically. In a lending context, a missed risky borrower represents credit loss; a false positive represents a lost origination fee. Adjusting the threshold is where domain knowledge and model output meet.

**Ensemble methods as variance reduction.** The neural network overfit noticeably (train accuracy 0.991, test 0.783), while the Random Forest generalized well despite similar complexity. The difference lies in the ensemble mechanism: by training 200 trees on bootstrap samples with random feature subsets, the forest averages out the high-variance behavior of individual trees. This confirmed the textbook intuition that bagging is particularly effective when base learners are unstable — and that more expressive models are not always better models.
